Perspective · March 2026

Content models are arguments about meaning

Every content model encodes a claim about what matters and what can be told apart. The trouble is that these claims are usually made by accident, in a hurry, by whoever set up the fields.

A content model looks like plumbing. It gets discussed as plumbing — field types, validation rules, reference cardinality — and it gets handed to whoever is closest to the CMS. But the moment you decide that a case study has a client and an article does not, you have made an argument about the shape of the world your organisation operates in. That argument will outlive the person who made it, and it will quietly constrain what anyone can say for years afterward.

This is the part that makes modelling difficult, and it is not a technical difficulty. Deciding whether two things are the same kind of thing requires knowing what you intend to do with them, and most organisations do not know that yet when the fields get created.

Structure precedes vocabulary

There is a persistent temptation to start with the words. Someone proposes a tagging scheme, everyone argues productively about terms for a week, and the resulting vocabulary gets bolted onto a structure that cannot support the distinctions it makes. The terms are fine. The structure cannot hold them.

Structure comes first because structure determines which distinctions are even available. If notes and essays share one type, no vocabulary can reliably separate them at query time — you can only annotate the difference and hope every author annotates consistently, which they will not.

A taxonomy applied to the wrong structure does not fail loudly. It degrades, slowly, into a set of labels nobody trusts.

Where types earn their keep

A separate type is justified when the thing behaves differently — different lifecycle, different required fields, different rendering, different place in a feed. It is not justified merely because the thing feels different. Feelings about content are a poor basis for schema, because they are not stable across a team and they change faster than the content does.

A note on granularity

Granularity failures are asymmetric. Splitting a type that should have stayed whole is recoverable — you merge, you migrate, you write a script and an apology. Merging types that should have stayed distinct destroys information you never captured, and no migration can recover a distinction that was never recorded.

What the reader actually experiences

None of this is visible to a reader, and that is exactly why it gets deferred. What the reader sees is whether the site can tell them what kind of thing they are looking at, whether related material is genuinely related, and whether a topic page gathers everything it should.

Which returns the whole problem to where it started. Deciding what kinds of things you publish is not preparation for the design work. It is the design work, conducted in a medium that happens to be schema rather than type.
